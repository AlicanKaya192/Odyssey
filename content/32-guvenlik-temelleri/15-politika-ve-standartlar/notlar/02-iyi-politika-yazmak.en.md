A thirty-page policy that nobody reads is not much different from having
no policy at all. What makes a good policy document:

## Short and clear

- One or two pages. Technical detail is left to standards and procedures.
- Instead of an unmeasurable sentence such as "necessary measures are
  taken", it says who does what.
- "Must" (mandatory) is kept apart from "should" (advice).

## Its sections

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Why does this policy exist? |
| Scope | Which people and which systems does it cover? |
| Roles | Who owns it, who applies it, who checks it? |
| Rules | The main principles |
| Exceptions | How are they requested, who approves? |
| Consequences | What happens if it is not followed? |
| Review | When and by whom? |

## Workable

- If a rule makes it impossible for people to do their job, it gets broken
  and nobody says so. The people who will apply a rule are asked before it
  is written.
- Every rule has an answer to "how will we measure that this is applied?".

## Announced and taught

- A new employee reads and accepts it in the first week.
- Changes are announced; the old version is archived, and the date and
  version number are written in the document.
- Awareness training explains why the policy exists instead of only reading
  out the rules.

## Common mistakes

- Copying another organisation's policy with the name changed: it
  describes another organisation's risks.
- Never reviewing it: the document talks about systems no longer in use.
- Having no exception path: rules get broken quietly.
