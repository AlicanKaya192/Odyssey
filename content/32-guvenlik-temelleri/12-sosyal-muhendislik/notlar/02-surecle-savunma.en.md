Even the best technical control cannot stop an employee who has been
persuaded. That is why most of the most effective controls against social
engineering are **processes**: rules written down as "we always check this
kind of request like this". If the rule is written, a person in a hurry
does not have to think; they follow the rule.

## Payments and bank account changes

- A supplier's bank account is changed **only** after checking by calling
  the registered phone number; the number in the e-mail is not called.
- Payments above a certain amount need the approval of two people (the
  two-person rule).
- A message saying "a secret purchase, don't tell anyone" is a warning sign
  on its own.

## The help desk

- For a password reset or an MFA device change, the caller's identity is
  checked in a way decided in advance (for example their manager's
  approval, a code sent to a registered device).
- Pressure such as "very urgent, the general manager is waiting" does not
  change the rule.

## Visitors and the building

- Visitors sign in and someone stays with them.
- Through a card door it is "one person, one card"; holding the door out of
  politeness is outside the rule.
- USB sticks that are found are not plugged into a computer; they are given
  to the security team.

## A reporting culture

- Reporting must be easy: one button, one number.
- The person who reports gets thanked, not punished. An employee who hides
  that they clicked means an attacker staying inside unnoticed for hours.
- The aim of an exercise is not to catch people but to build a habit;
  results are shared as a whole, not person by person.

## The ethical line

A phishing exercise is only run with the organisation's written
permission, with a clear scope and with respect for the staff's personal
data. Even sending a friend a fake login page "as a joke" can be a crime.
