**Idea:** Work with fractions of the class without ever turning to head counts. "$\frac{1}{3}$ of the girls" is $\frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{5}$ of the class: a fraction of a fraction is a product.

**Step 1 — Girls with glasses, as a fraction of the class.**

$$
\frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{5} = \frac{1}{5}
$$

**Step 2 — Boys with glasses.** The boys are $1 - \frac{3}{5} = \frac{2}{5}$ of the class:

$$
\frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = \frac{2}{20} = \frac{1}{10}
$$

**Step 3 — Add.** Both fractions are now parts of the same whole (the class), so they can be added:

$$
\frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{10} = \frac{2}{10} + \frac{1}{10} = \frac{3}{10}
$$

**Step 4 — The number of girls.** $\frac{1}{5}$ of the class: $\frac{1}{5} \cdot 30 = 6$.

**Why the same result?** Both routes do the same multiplications; the first multiplied by $30$ early, this one left it to the end. What matters is that the fractions being added refer to **the same whole**.

**Answer:** $6$ and $\dfrac{3}{10}$.
