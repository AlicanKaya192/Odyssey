In real life, situations are not always as clear as "there is permission"
or "there is none". In all the examples below, the right behaviour comes
from the same question: **is there anything in writing that authorises me
to do this?**

## Example situations

| Situation | Right behaviour |
|---|---|
| While using a site normally, you see someone else's data | Look no further, do not save it; report it to the site (the Responsible Disclosure section) |
| A friend says "try my site" | Is the site theirs? Settle the scope and timing in writing |
| Your employer asks you to test a competitor's site | Not done without the competitor's permission; the employer's is not enough |
| During a test you notice you can reach an out-of-scope machine | You stop and ask for written permission to widen the scope |
| You find a password file during a test | You report that it exists; you do not try or copy the passwords |
| A service in scope slows down during the test | You stop and tell the emergency contact |
| In a competition (CTF) you can reach an address outside the target system | You do not go beyond the targets the rules allow |

## Your own lab

You do not need someone else's system to learn. Working on your own
computer, in virtual machines you set up yourself, or with toy examples
like the ones in this path is both legal and more instructive: you can
break everything and rebuild it. If you join a competition or a training
environment, read its rules; permission covers only the targets the rules
name.

## Honesty in the report

- Write the impact of a finding as far as you measured it; do not add
  unverified claims such as "everything can be taken over".
- Do not present what you did not try as tried.
- Do not hide the risks the test itself created (such as stopping a service
  briefly); write them in the report.
- The report is shared only with the people named in the contract.
