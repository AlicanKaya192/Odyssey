# Derivatives: Rate of Change

A car's speedometer shows the speed at that moment: not how many
kilometres it covers in an hour, but how fast it is going **right now**.
"Right now" is not a stretch of time but an instant, and no distance is
covered in an instant. So where does that number come from? The answer is
the derivative: the limit of the average speed over very short intervals.

Machine learning asks the same question: if I change a weight very
slightly, how much does the loss change? This number tells us both which
way to go and how far. In this section we will build the derivative from
its definition, see what it tells us and take a first learning step.

Prerequisite: the Limits and Continuity section, and The Coordinate Plane
and Lines (slope) in the Foundational Mathematics module.

## Average rate of change

A stone is dropped; the distance it falls in $t$ seconds is about
$s(t) = 5t^2$ metres. Its average speed between second $1$ and second $3$:

$$
\frac{s(3) - s(1)}{3 - 1} = \frac{45 - 5}{2} = 20 \text{ m/s}
$$

In general, the **average rate of change** on the interval $[a, b]$ is:

$$
\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}
$$

Its geometric meaning is familiar: the slope of the line joining $(a,
f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ on the graph, the **secant**.

## Instantaneous rate of change

We want the stone's speed at exactly $t = 3$. Let us move the second point
towards $3$: take the interval $[3, 3 + h]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{s(3 + h) - s(3)}{h} &= \frac{5(9 + 6h + h^2) - 45}{h} \\
&= \frac{30h + 5h^2}{h} = 30 + 5h
\end{aligned}
$$

| $h$ | $1$ | $0.1$ | $0.01$ | $0.001$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| average speed | $35$ | $30.5$ | $30.05$ | $30.005$ |

As $h \to 0$, the average speed goes to $30$. The stone's speed at $t = 3$
is $30$ m/s. This is the **instantaneous rate of change**; as the second
point moves closer to the first, the slope of the secant turns into the
slope of the **tangent**.

## The definition of the derivative

The **derivative** of $f$ at the point $x$:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

If the limit exists, $f$ is **differentiable** at that point. It is read:
$f'(x)$ "f prime of x", $\dfrac{df}{dx}$ or $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ "d f by d x".
The second notation is Leibniz's: $dx$ is "a very small change in $x$",
$dy$ the change that goes with it.

**The derivative is a function.** For $f(x) = x^2$, apply the definition
at a general $x$:

$$
\frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h \;\to\; 2x
$$

So $f'(x) = 2x$. The slope at every point from one formula: $2$ at
$x = 1$, $6$ at $x = 3$, $-4$ at $x = -2$.

## The tangent line

Since the derivative is the slope of the tangent, the tangent at $x = a$
in point–slope form is:

$$
y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)
$$

$y = x^2$, $a = 1$: $f(1) = 1$, $f'(1) = 2$, and the tangent is
$y - 1 = 2(x - 1)$, that is, $y = 2x - 1$. The tangent is the line that
fits the curve best right around the point: at $x = 1.1$ the curve is at
$1.21$ and the tangent at $1.2$.

**Units.** The unit of $f'$ is "output unit per input unit". If distance
is in metres and time in seconds, speed is in m/s. If the loss and the
weight are plain numbers, the derivative says "when the weight goes up by
one unit, how many units the loss goes up by".

## Basic derivatives

Derivatives found (once) from the definition; the next section
generalises them with rules:

| $f(x)$ | $f'(x)$ |
|---|---|
| $c$ (constant) | $0$ |
| $x$ | $1$ |
| $x^2$ | $2x$ |
| $x^3$ | $3x^2$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ |
| $\ln x$ | $\dfrac{1}{x}$ |

A pattern shows: the derivative of $x^n$ is $n x^{n-1}$. And $e^x$ is the
only function equal to its own derivative (apart from constant
multiples); that is exactly what is special about $e$.

**Sums and constant multiples.** From the limit rules: the derivative of
a sum is taken term by term, and a constant factor comes out. If $f(x) =
3x^2 + 5x - 7$, then $f'(x) = 6x + 5$.

**ReLU's corner.** $\max(0, x)$ has no derivative at $x = 0$: the slope
is $0$ from the left and $1$ from the right. In practice libraries take
the derivative there to be $0$; since an input almost never lands on
exactly $0$, the choice does not change the result.

## Numerical derivatives

When there is no formula, the derivative is estimated with a small $h$.
Instead of the forward difference $\dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$, the
**central difference** is much more accurate:

$$
f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}
$$

$f(x) = x^2$, $x = 3$, $h = 0.1$: the forward difference gives $6.1$, the
central difference exactly $6$. Deep learning libraries compute
derivatives from formulas; numerical derivatives are used for **gradient
checking**: if the two results disagree, there is a bug in the code.

At $w = 5$, $L'(5) = 4$. A positive slope says "going right raises the
loss", so let us go left. One step with **learning rate** $\eta = 0.1$:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \, L'(w) = 5 - 0.1 \cdot 4 = 4.6
$$

The loss dropped from $5$ to $L(4.6) = 3.56$. Taking this step over and
over is **gradient descent**; we will see it in full in the Gradient
Descent section. The size of the slope helps too: far from the minimum
the slope is large and so is the step; closer in, the slope shrinks and
so do the steps.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing average and instantaneous speed.** The average over
  $[1, 3]$ is $20$; the instantaneous speed at $t = 3$ is $30$.
- **Expanding $f(x + h)$ wrongly in the definition.** $(x + h)^2 = x^2 +
  2xh + h^2$, not $x^2 + h^2$.
- **Setting $h$ to zero before cancelling.** In $\frac{2xh + h^2}{h}$,
  cancel $h$ first; otherwise $\frac{0}{0}$ remains.
- **Confusing the derivative with the value.** $f'(3) = 6$ is a slope;
  $f(3) = 9$ is a height.

## Summary

- Average rate of change $\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$: the slope of the secant.
- Derivative $f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$: the instantaneous rate of change, the slope of the tangent.
- Tangent: $y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$.
- $f' > 0$ increasing, $f' < 0$ decreasing, $f' = 0$ a horizontal tangent.
- $(x^n)' = n x^{n-1}$, $(e^x)' = e^x$, $(\ln x)' = \dfrac{1}{x}$; the derivative of a sum is taken term by term.
- No derivative at a corner, a discontinuity or a vertical tangent; a differentiable function is continuous.
- Numerical derivatives with the central difference; gradient checking.
- The learning step $w \leftarrow w - \eta L'(w)$: go against the slope.
