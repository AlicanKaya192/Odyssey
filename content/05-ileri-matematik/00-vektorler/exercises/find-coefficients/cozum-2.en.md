**Idea:** The two equations from the first method can also be solved without substitution. If we multiply one equation by a suitable number and subtract it from the other, one of the unknowns disappears (elimination).

**Step 1 — Set up the equations.** Expanding the left side and matching components (as in the first method):

$$
\begin{aligned}
a + 3b &= 7 \\
2a + b &= 9
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2 — Make the $a$ coefficients equal.** The second equation has $2a$. Multiplying the first equation by 2 gives $2a$ there too. Multiplying both sides of an equation by the same number keeps it true:

$$
2a + 6b = 14
$$

**Step 3 — Subtract.** Subtract the second equation from this one. Since $2a - 2a = 0$, $a$ disappears:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2a + 6b) - (2a + b) &= 14 - 9 \\
5b &= 5 \\
b &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 4 — Find $a$.** Put $b = 1$ into the first equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a + 3 \cdot 1 &= 7 \\
a &= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

**Check:** The second equation must hold too: $2 \cdot 4 + 1 = 9$. ✓

**Why this method?** As the number of unknowns grows, substitution gets messy, while elimination repeats the same move every time. The Gaussian elimination section of MAT 2 is this method organised for large systems.

**Answer:** $a = 4$, $b = 1$.
